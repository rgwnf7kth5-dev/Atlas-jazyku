While most examples are ancient, a small number are [[Middle Ages|medieval]] or modern.<ref name="Adiego2024" /> In some cases the evidence is too limited to determine whether the marks represent a true writing system, a form of [[proto-writing]], or a set of non-linguistic symbols; modern artistic traditions such as [[asemic writing]] likewise imitate the appearance of writing while intentionally withholding stable linguistic meaning.<ref>{{cite book
 |last=Schwenger |first=Peter
 |title=Asemic: The Art of Writing
 |publisher=University of Minnesota Press
 |date=2019
 |isbn=978-1-5179-0697-9
 |url=https://www.upress.umn.edu/9781517906979/asemic/
 |access-date=2026-02-07
}}</ref>

Difficulties in decipherment commonly arise from one or more of the following: the absence of [[Multilingualism|bilingual]] texts or other external "anchors" (such as securely identifiable names or dates); uncertain or unknown underlying languages (including possible [[language isolate]]s); small [[Text corpus|corpora]]; and damage or loss of archaeological context needed to test proposed readings against use and genre.<ref name="Adiego2024" /> In some corpora, an additional difficulty is uncertainty over whether the signs constitute writing at all (as has often been argued for the [[Vinča symbols]]).<ref>{{cite book

 |location=Warsaw, Poland
 |publisher=De Gruyter Open Poland
 |date=2016
 |pages=53–142
}}</ref>

==Inclusion criteria==
This list includes writing systems and sign systems that are commonly described in reliable secondary sources as undeciphered, not fully deciphered, or of uncertain decipherment. Items of disputed authenticity or disputed status as writing are explicitly identified as such.

==Proto-writing==
Certain forms of [[proto-writing]] remain undeciphered and, because of limited evidence and uncertain linguistic affiliation, may never be deciphered.

==American scripts==
===Andean region===
* [[Quipu]] – Knotted-cord recording devices used in the [[Inca Empire]] and by several earlier Andean societies such as the [[Wari culture|Wari]]. Possible Proto-Quipus have been found in [[Paracas culture|Paracas]] and [[Caral–Supe civilization|Caral-Supe]], though these remain controversial. Quipus continued to be used in certain regions during the early colonial period and, in limited contexts, into the modern era. They are widely accepted as record-keeping devices; whether they constitute "writing" (and what kind) remains debated. Some recent scholarship argues that particular colonial-period quipu assemblages display features consistent with logosyllabic encoding and proposes tentative readings of specific elements; however, these claims remain controversial and do not represent a broadly accepted decipherment.<ref>{{cite journal
 |last=Hyland |first=Sabine
 |date=2017
 |title=Writing with Twisted Cords: The Inscriptive Capacity of Andean Khipus
 |journal=Current Anthropology
 |volume=58
 |issue=3
 |pages=412–419

Many Mesoamerican glyphic traditions remain only partly understood, with the [[Maya script|Classic/Lowland Maya script]] being the best-attested and most extensively deciphered. Other regional traditions (including those often grouped under "Mixteca-Puebla" or pictorial-historical codex traditions ultimately descended from the Teotihuacan tradition, most notably represented by [[Aztec script|Aztec hieroglyphs]] and [[Mixtec writing|Mixtec hieroglyphs]]) have been interpreted in the literature to varying extents, but are not universally recognised as a writing system, with some researchers considering it a mostly non-linguistic form of expression.<ref>{{Cite book |last1=Boone |first1=Elizabeth Hill |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=JP-BXBD54uwC&dq=writing+without+words&pg=PA233 |title=Writing Without Words: Alternative Literacies in Mesoamerica and the Andes |last2=Mignolo |first2=Walter |date=1994 |publisher=Duke University Press |isbn=978-0-8223-1388-5 |language=en}}</ref> Yet, in recent years{{As of?|date=May 2026}} new research has appeared confirming the existence of logosyllabic elements ("glyphs") intermixed with pictorial images.<ref>{{Cite book |last=Whittaker |first=Gordon |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=bUrbzQEACAAJ |title=Deciphering Aztec Hieroglyphs: A Guide to Nahuatl Writing |date=2021 |publisher=University of California Press |isbn=978-0-520-38037-0 |language=en}}</ref><ref name=":0" /> Nevertheless, new approaches have emerged, integrating the pictorial images as key in the linguistic interpretation of all elements in the codices, alongside the [[Logogram|logograms]] and [[Phonogram (linguistics)|phonograms]]. These pictorial images would represent greater units of meaning, namely semantograms, also called embedded meanings, than a lexeme (logograms) or a sound (phonogram) which would become of paramount importance to achieve a coherent linguistic message in the codices.<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Corona |first=Alonso Rodrigo Zamora |date=2022-01-01 |title=Towards a Complex Theory of Writing: The Case of Aztec and Mixtec Codices. |url=https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi:10.4000%2Fsignata.3866?sid=ebsco:plink:crawler&id=ebsco:doi:10.4000%2Fsignata.3866 |journal=Signata |volume=13 |language=en |issue=13 |pages=350 |doi=10.4000/signata.3866 |issn=2032-9806}}</ref> Thus, Mixteca-Puebla writing would constitute a form of non-linear writing.

==Non writing systems==
[[False writing system]]s are glyphs that appear to be writing but are not. False writing cannot be deciphered because it has no stable [[semantics|semantic]] meaning. This includes many forms of [[asemic writing]] created for artistic purposes. A prominent example is the ''[[Codex Seraphinianus]]''.

Another related concept is that of undeciphered [[cryptogram]]s or [[cipher]] messages. These are not writing systems ''per se'', but enciphered forms of texts written in known systems. Some have become famous and are listed in [[list of ciphertexts]].
