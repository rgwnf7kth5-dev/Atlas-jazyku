The word "'''orange'''" is a [[noun]] and an [[adjective]] in the [[English language]]. In both cases, it refers primarily to the [[orange (fruit)|orange fruit]] and the [[orange (colour)|color orange]], but has many other derivative meanings.

The word is derived from a [[Dravidian language]], and it passed through numerous other languages including [[Sanskrit]], [[Persian language|Persian]], and [[Old French]] before reaching the English language. The earliest uses of the word in English refer to the fruit, and the color was later named after the fruit. Before the English-speaking world was exposed to the fruit, the color was referred to as "yellow-red" (''[[wikt:geoluread|geoluread]]'' in [[Old English]]) or "red-yellow".<ref>{{cite book |title=Symbols and their hidden meanings |last=Kenner |first=T.A. |year=2006 |publisher=Thunders Mouth |location=New York |isbn=1-56025-949-3 |page=11}}</ref>

"Orange" has no true [[rhyme]]. There are several [[half rhyme]]s or near-rhymes, as well as some [[proper noun]]s and [[compound word]]s or phrases that rhyme with it. This lack of rhymes has inspired many humorous poems and songs.

== Etymology ==
The word "orange" entered [[Middle English]] from Old French and [[Anglo-Norman language|Anglo-Norman]] ''orenge''.<ref name=OED>{{cite encyclopedia | year = 2013 | title = orange ''n''.<sup>1</sup> and ''adj''.<sup>1</sup> | encyclopedia = Oxford English Dictionary online | publisher = Oxford University Press | location = Oxford | url =http://www.oed.com/view/Entry/132163 |access-date=2013-09-30 |url-access=subscription}}</ref> The earliest recorded use of the word in English is from the 13th century and referred to the fruit. The first recorded use of "orange" as a colour name in English was in 1502, in a description of clothing purchased for [[Margaret Tudor]].<ref name="StClair2">{{Cite book|title=The Secret Lives of Colour|last=St. Clair|first=Kassia|publisher=John Murray|year=2016|isbn=9781473630819|location=London|pages=88|oclc=936144129}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=Sv2NRSdP58kC |title=Elephant's Breath & London Smoke: Historical Colour Names, Definitions & Uses|last=Salisbury|first=Deb|date=2009|publisher=Five Rivers Chapmanry|isbn=9780973927825|pages=148|language=en}}</ref> Other sources cite the first recorded use as 1512, in a [[Will (law)|will]] now filed with the [[Public Record Office]].<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.oed.com/dictionary/orange-colour_n|title=orange colour – orange color, n. (and adj.)|website=Oxford English Dictionary|publisher=OED|access-date=6 March 2024}}</ref><ref>{{Cite book|last1=Maerz|first1=Aloys John|author2=Morris Rea Paul|year=1930|title=A Dictionary of Color|location=New York|publisher=McGraw-Hill|page=200}}</ref> It is generally thought that Old French [[calque]]d the [[Italian language|Italian]] ''melarancio'' ("fruit of the orange tree", with ''mela'' "fruit") as ''pume orenge'' (with ''pume'' "fruit").<ref>{{cite book |title=Word Histories and Mysteries: From Abracadabra to Zeus |url=https://archive.org/details/wordhistoriesmys00dict |url-access=limited |year=2004 |publisher=Houghton Mifflin |location=Boston |isbn=0-618-45450-0 |page=[https://archive.org/details/wordhistoriesmys00dict/page/n217 201]}}</ref><ref>{{cite encyclopedia |year=2000 |title=orange |encyclopedia=The American Heritage Dictionary of the English Language |publisher=Houghton Mifflin |location=New York |isbn=0-395-82517-2 |url=https://archive.org/details/americanheritage0000unse_a1o7 }}</ref> Although ''pume orenge'' is attested earlier than ''melarancio'' in available written sources, [[Lexicography|lexicographers]] believe that the Italian word is actually older.<ref name=OED />

The word ultimately derives from a Dravidian language – possibly [[Tamil language|Tamil]] {{lang|ta|நாரம்}} ''nāram'' or [[Telugu language|Telugu]] {{lang|te|నారింజ}} ''nāriṃja'' — via Sanskrit {{lang|sa|नारङ्ग}} ''nāraṅgaḥ'' "orange tree". From there the word entered [[Persian language|Persian]] {{lang|fa|نارنگ}} ''nārang'' and then [[Arabic language|Arabic]] {{lang|ar|نارنج}} ''nāranj''.<ref name=OED /> The initial ''n'' was lost through [[rebracketing]] in Italian and French, though some varieties of Arabic lost the ''n'' earlier.<ref name=OED /> Compare Spanish [[cognate]] [[wikt:naranja|''naranja'']] with initial ''n''

The [[Orange, Vaucluse|place named Orange]] has a separate etymology. The Roman-Celtic settlement was founded in 36 or 35 [[Anno Domini|BC]] and originally named Arausio, after a [[Celts|Celtic]] [[Arausio (god)|water god]].<ref>{{cite book |title=A Dictionary of the Roman Empire |last=Bunson |first=Matthew |year=1995 |publisher=Oxford University Press |location=Oxford and New York |isbn=0-19-510233-9 |page=23}}</ref> The [[Principality of Orange]] was named for this place and not for the color. Some time after the sixteenth century, though, the color orange was adopted as a [[canting arms|canting]] symbol of the [[House of Orange-Nassau]].<ref>{{cite book |title=Spanish Vocabulary: An Etymological Approach |url=https://archive.org/details/spanishvocabular00brod |url-access=limited |last=Brodsky |first=David |year=2008 |publisher=University of Texas Press |location=Austin |isbn=978-0-292-71668-1 |page=[https://archive.org/details/spanishvocabular00brod/page/n273 257]}}</ref> The color eventually came to be associated with [[Protestantism]], as a result of the participation by the House of Orange on the Protestant side in the [[French Wars of Religion]], the [[Williamite War in Ireland]], and the [[Netherlands|Dutch]] [[Eighty Years' War]].<ref>{{cite book |title=Protestant Nations Redefined: Changing Perceptions of National Identity in the Rhetoric of the English, Dutch, and Swedish Public Churches, 1685–1772 |last=Ihalainen |first=Pasi |year=2005 |publisher=BRILL |location=Leiden and Boston |isbn=90-04-14485-4 |page=348}}</ref>

==Pronunciation==
{{further|English-language vowel changes before historical /r/#Mergers of /ɒr/ and /ɔr/}}
With ''forest'', ''warrant'', ''horrible'', etc., ''orange'' forms a class of English words where the North American pronunciation of what is pronounced as {{IPA|/ɒ/}}, the vowel in ''lot'', in British [[Received Pronunciation]] varies between the vowel in ''north'' ({{IPA|/ɔ/}} or {{IPA|/o/}} depending on the [[cot–caught merger|''cot''–''caught'' merger]]) and that in ''lot'' ({{IPA|/ɑ/}} or {{IPA|/ɒ/}} depending on the [[father–bother merger|''father''–''bother'' merger]]). The former is more common while the latter is mainly found on the [[East Coast of the United States]].<ref>{{Accents of English|hide2=y}} Pages 136–137, 476, 479, 525.</ref> While many dictionaries of North American English include the ''north'' pronunciation as the primary or only variant,<ref>{{cite LPD|3}}</ref><ref>{{cite EPD|18}}</ref><ref>{{cite DPCE|2|page=934}}</ref><ref>{{cite American Heritage Dictionary|orange|access-date=2023-01-09}}</ref><ref>{{cite Dictionary.com|orange|access-date=2023-01-09}}</ref><ref>{{cite book|last1=de Wolf|first1=Gaelan Dodds|last2=Gregg|first2=Robert J.|last3=Harris|first3=Barbara P.|last4=Scargill|first4=Matthew H.|year=1997|title=Gage Canadian Dictionary|location=Toronto|publisher=Gage|page=1038|isbn=0-7715-1981-8}}</ref> [[Merriam-Webster]] lists the ''lot'' variant first and glosses the ''north'' variant as "chiefly Northern & Midland" for ''orange'' but not for other words in the class (''forest'' etc.).<ref>{{cite Merriam-Webster|orange|access-date=2023-01-09}}</ref> Its ''[[Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary|Collegiate Dictionary]]'' listed ''north'' first until the 1973 eighth edition,<ref>{{cite book|title=Webster's New Collegiate Dictionary|year=1973|location=Springfield, Mass.|publisher=G. & C. Merriam|page=806|isbn=0-87779-338-7}}</ref> but has flipped the order since the 1983 ninth edition.<ref>{{cite book|title=Webster's Ninth New Collegiate Dictionary|year=1983|location=Springfield, Mass.|publisher=Merriam-Webster|page=829|isbn=0-87779-508-8}}</ref> Merriam-Webster is also unique in including monosyllabic variants ({{IPAc-en|ɑːr|n|dʒ|,_|ɔːr|n|dʒ}}).

== Rhyme ==<!-- This section is linked from [[Sniglet]] -->
No common [[English language|English]] word is a full rhyme for "orange", though there are [[half rhyme]]s, such as "hinge", "lozenge", "syringe", and "porridge".<ref>{{cite book |title=Song and Significance: Virtues and Vices of Vocal Translation |last=Gorlée |first=Dinda L. |year=2005 |publisher=Rodopi |location=Amsterdam |isbn=90-420-1687-6 |page=199}}</ref> Slang and otherwise uncommon examples exist. Although this property is not unique to the word—one study of 5,411 one-syllable English words found 80 [[List of English words without rhymes|words with no rhymes]]<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Lawler |first1=John |year=2006 |title=The Data Fetishist's Guide to Rime Coherence |journal=Style |volume=40 |issue=1&2}}</ref>—the lack of rhyme for "orange" has garnered significant attention, and inspired many [[Light poetry|humorous verses]].

Although "[[wikt:en:sporange|sporange]]", a variant of "[[sporangium]]", is an [[eye rhyme]] for "orange", it is not a true rhyme as its second syllable is pronounced with an [[vowel reduction|unreduced vowel]] {{IPA|[-ændʒ]}}, and often [[stress (linguistics)|stressed]].<ref>{{cite encyclopedia | year = 1989 | title = sporange, ''n''. | encyclopedia = Oxford English Dictionary, second edition | publisher = Oxford University Press | location = Oxford | isbn = 0-19-861186-2}}</ref>

There are a number of proper nouns which rhyme or nearly rhyme with "orange", including [[The Blorenge]], a mountain in [[Wales]], and [[Gorringe (disambiguation)|Gorringe]], a [[surname]]. US Naval Commander [[Henry Honychurch Gorringe]], the captain of the {{USS|Gettysburg|1858|6}}, who discovered [[Gorringe Ridge]] in 1875,<ref>{{cite web|url=http://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/history/timeline/timeline.html#breakthrough |title=History of NOAA Ocean Exploration: The Breakthrough Years (1866–1922) |publisher=Oceanexplorer.noaa.gov |access-date=2009-07-03}}</ref> led [[Arthur Guiterman]] to quip in "Local Note":<ref>{{cite book |title=Gaily the Troubadour |last=Guiterman |first=Arthur |year=1936 |publisher=E.P. Dutton |location=Boston |oclc=1395889}}</ref>

The slang word "blorange", a hair color between blond and orange, is a rhyme. It is attested from the early 2000s and appears in fashion-related media from about 2017.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.dictionary.com/e/fashion/blorange/?itm_source=parsely-api |title=blorange |date=July 1, 2019 |website=Dictionary.com |access-date=October 19, 2022}}</ref>

Various linguistic or poetic devices provide for rhymes in some accents.

[[Compound word]]s or phrases may give true or near rhymes. Examples include "door-hinge", "torn hinge", "or inch", and "a wrench".
