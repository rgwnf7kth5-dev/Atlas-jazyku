'''Kusunda''' or '''Kusanda''' ([[endonym]] {{Lang|kgg|Gemehaq gipan}} {{IPA|kgg|gemʰjaχ|lang|Gyani_Maiya-kgg_mehaq_1.wav}}{{IPA|kgg|gipən||Gyani Maiya-kgg gepan 1.wav}}<ref name="Watters"/>) is a [[language isolate]] spoken by a few among the [[Kusunda people]] in western and central [[Nepal]]. As of 2023, it only has a single fluent speaker, [[Kamala Sen-Khatri]],<ref>{{Cite web |last=Bhattarai |first=Sewa |date=2023-05-13 |title=The last of the Kusunda |url=https://nepalitimes.com/multimedia/the-last-of-the-kusunda |access-date=2023-05-28 |website=nepalitimes.com}}</ref> although there are efforts underway to keep the language alive.<ref>{{Cite web |last=McDougall |first=Eileen |title=The language that doesn't use 'no' |url=https://www.bbc.com/future/article/20220804-kusunda-the-language-isolate-with-no-word-for-no |access-date=2022-08-11 |website=www.bbc.com |language=en}}</ref> There were 23 native speakers according to the [[2021 Nepal census]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=caste-ethnicity-report {{!}} national_population and housing_census_year results |url=https://censusnepal.cbs.gov.np/results/downloads/caste-ethnicity?type=data |access-date=2025-04-23 |website=censusnepal.cbs.gov.np}}</ref>

==History==
[[File:OpenSpeaks-Kgg-Gejmehac Gipan-Gyani Maiya Sen Kusunda-Uday Raj Aaley-Kusunda language endangerment.webm|thumb|[[Kusunda people|Kusunda]] elder [[Gyani Maiya Sen-Kusunda]] discussing with [[Uday Raj Aaley]] the endangerment of Kusunda in documentary ''Gyani Maiya'' (2019)]]

For decades the Kusunda language was thought to be on the verge of extinction, with little hope of ever knowing it well. The little material that could be gleaned from the memories of former speakers suggested that the language was an isolate, but, without much evidence, it was often classified along with its neighbors as [[Tibeto-Burman languages|Tibeto-Burman]]. However, in 2004 three Kusundas, Gyani Maya Sen, Prem Bahadur Shahi and Kamala Singh,<ref name=3Kusundas>{{cite web |url=http://listserv.emich.edu/cgi-bin/wa?A2=ind0410&L=endangered-languages-l&P=439 |archive-url=https://archive.today/20121210104535/http://listserv.emich.edu/cgi-bin/wa?A2=ind0410&L=endangered-languages-l&P=439 |archive-date=2012-12-10 |title=Kusunda language does not fall in any family: Study |access-date=2007-09-12 |last=Rana |first=B.K. |date=2004-10-12 |work=email with pasted news article |publisher=Himalayan News Service, Lalitpur, 2004-10-10 }}</ref> were brought to [[Kathmandu]] for help with citizenship papers. There, members of [[Tribhuvan University]] discovered that one of them, a native of Sakhi VDC in southern [[Rolpa District]], was a fluent speaker of the language. Several of her relatives were also discovered to be fluent. In 2005 there were known to be seven or eight fluent speakers of the language, the youngest in her thirties.<ref name="Watters"/> However the language is [[moribund language|moribund]], with no children learning it, since all Kusunda speakers have married outside their ethnicity.<ref name="Watters" />

It was presumed that the language became extinct with the death of Rajamama Kusunda on 19 April 2018.<ref>{{Cite news|url=http://kathmandupost.ekantipur.com/news/2018-04-19/rajamama-lone-kusunda-language-speaker-dies.html|title=Rajamama, lone Kusunda language speaker, dies|access-date=2018-06-18|language=en}}</ref> However, [[Gyani Maiya Sen-Kusunda|Gyani Maiya Sen]] and her sister [[Kamala Sen-Khatri]] contributed in further data collection, language training and revival of the language.<ref>{{Cite report|doi = 10.17613/1zy2-k376|year = 2019|last1 = Aaley|first1 = Uday Raj|last2 = Bodt|first2 = Timotheus (Tim) Adrianus|title = New data on Kusunda| publisher=Humanities Commons }}</ref> The sisters, together with author and researcher [[Uday Raj Aaley]], have been teaching the language to interested children and adults.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://kathmandupost.com/national/2019/01/04/resuscitating-dying-kusunda-language|title=Resuscitating dying Kusunda language|date=4 January 2019|website=The Kathmandu Post|access-date=17 September 2019}}</ref>

Aaley, the facilitator and Kusunda-language teacher, has written the book ''Kusunda Tribe and Dictionary''.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://kathmandupost.com/art-entertainment/2017/08/01/book-that-traces-kusunda-tribes-history-hits-shelves|title=Book that traces Kusunda tribe's history hits shelves|date=1 August 2017|website=The Kathmandu Post|access-date=17 September 2019}}</ref> The book has a compilation of more than 1000 words from the Kusunda language. Uday Raj Aaley and Timotheus A. Bodt published “New Kusunda data: A list of 250 concepts,” in 2020.<ref>[https://calc.hypotheses.org/2446 New Lexical Data for the Kusunda Language]</ref> Nepal Archive has offered a free online basic to advanced level language lessons in their website.<ref>[https://www.archivenepal.org/kusundacourse Learn Kusunda Language]</ref>

==Classification==
[[David E. Watters]] published a mid-sized grammatical description of the language, plus vocabulary ([[#Watters2005|Watters 2005]]), although further works have been published since.<ref>[[#stance|Donohue & Gautam (2013)]].</ref> He argued that Kusunda is indeed a language isolate, not just genealogically but also lexically, grammatically and phonologically distinct from its neighbors. This would imply that Kusunda is a remnant of the languages spoken in northern India before the influx of Tibeto-Burman- and [[Indo-Iranian languages|Indo-Iranian]]-speaking peoples; however it is not classified as a [[Munda languages|Munda]] nor a [[Dravidian languages|Dravidian]] language. It thus joins [[Burushaski]], [[Nihali language|Nihali]] and (potentially) the substrate of the [[Vedda language]] in the list of South Asian languages that do not fall into the main categories of Indo-European, Dravidian, [[Sino-Tibetan languages|Sino-Tibetan]], and [[Austroasiatic languages|Austroasiatic]].

Before the recent discovery of active Kusunda speakers there had been several attempts to link the language to an established language family. B.K. Rana (2002) maintained that Kusunda was a [[Tibeto-Burman languages|Tibeto-Burman language]] as traditionally classified. [[Merritt Ruhlen]] argued for a relationship with [[Juwoi language|Juwoi]] and other [[Andamanese languages]]; and for a larger [[Indo-Pacific languages|Indo-Pacific language family]], with them and other languages, including [[Nihali language|Nihali]].<ref>{{Cite journal

Others have linked Kusunda to [[Munda languages|Munda]] (see [[David E. Watters|Watters]] 2005); [[Yeniseian languages|Yeniseian]] (Gurov 1989); [[Burushaski language|Burushaski]] and [[Caucasian languages|Caucasian]] (Reinhard and Toba 1970; this would be a variant of Gurov's proposal if [[Sino-Caucasian]] were accepted); and the [[Nihali language|Nihali]] isolate in central India (Fleming 1996, Whitehouse 1997). More recently a relationship between Kusunda, Yeniseian and Burushaski has been proposed.<ref>van Driem, George (2014). 'A Prehistoric Thoroughfare between the Ganges and the Himalayas'. In: Jamir, Tiatoshi/Hazarika, Manjil eds 50 Years after Daojali-Hading: Emerging Perspectives in the Archaeology of Northeast India. New Delhi: Research India Press. 60–98.</ref>

==Phonology==

===Vowels===
Phonetically, Kusunda has six vowels in two [[vowel harmony|harmonic]] groups, which are arguably three vowels phonemically: a word will normally have vowels from the upper (pink, italic) or lower (green) set, but not both simultaneously. There are very few words that consistently have either always upper or always lower vowels; most words may be pronounced either way, though those with [[uvular consonant]]s require the lower set (as in many languages). There are a few words with no uvular consonants that still bar such dual pronunciations, though these generally only feature the distinction in careful enunciation.<ref name="Watters" />

===Consonants===
Kusunda consonants seem to only contrast the active articulator, not where that articulator makes contact. For example, apical consonants may be [[dental consonant|dental]], [[alveolar consonant|alveolar]], [[retroflex consonant|retroflex]], or [[palatal consonant|palatal]]: {{IPA|/t/}} is dental {{IPA|[t̪]}} before {{IPA|/i/}}, alveolar {{IPA|[t͇]}} before {{IPA|/e, ə, u/}}, retroflex {{IPA|[ʈ]}} before {{IPA|/o, a/}}, and palatal {{IPA|[c]}} when there is a following uvular, as in {{IPA|[coq]}} ~ {{IPA|[t͇ok]}} ('we').<ref name="Watters" />

In addition, many consonants vary between [[stop consonant|stops]] and [[fricative consonant|fricative]]s; for instance, {{IPA|/p/}} seems to surface as {{IPA|[b]}} between vowels, while {{IPA|/b/}} surfaces as {{IPA|[β]}} in the same environment. [[Aspiration (phonetics)|Aspiration]] appears to be recent to the language. Kusunda also lacks the [[retroflex consonant]] phonemes that are common to the region, and is unique in the region in having [[uvular consonant]]s.<ref name="Watters" />

==Pronouns==

Kusunda has several cases, marked on nouns and pronouns, three of which are the [[nominative]] (Kusunda, unlike its neighbors, has no [[ergativity]]), [[genitive]], and [[accusative case|accusative]].<ref name="Watters">[[#Watters2005|Watters (2005)]].</ref>

Other case suffixes include '''-ma''' "together with", '''-lage''' "for", '''-əna''' "from", '''-ga, -gə''' "at, in".

There are also [[demonstrative pronoun]]s '''na''' and '''ta'''. Although it is not clear what the difference between them is, it may be [[animacy]].

Subjects may be marked on the verb, though when they are they may either be prefixed or suffixed. An example with ''am'' "eat", which is more regular than many verbs, in the present tense (''-ən'') is,

==Negation==
Donohue, Gautam & Pokharel (2014) argue that Kusunda lacks an unambiguous means of negating declarative verbal main clauses, with the suffix {{Lang|kgg|-u}} marking a more general [[Irrealis mood|irrealis]], with negation as one of its possible functions,<ref name="Donohue2014">{{cite journal |last1=Donohue |first1=Mark |last2=Gautam |first2=Bhojraj |last3=Pokharel |first3=Madhav |title=Negation and nominalization in Kusunda |journal=[[Language (journal)|Language]] |volume=90 |issue=3 |pages=737–745 |year=2014 |doi=10.1353/lan.2014.0054}}</ref> contrary to the analysis in Watters et al. (2006), which had described several negative verbal suffixes, such as {{Lang|kgg|-aˤu, -daˤu, -wa}}.{{Sfn|Watters|Yadava|pp=71-75|Pokharel|Prasain|2006}}

[[Imperative mood|Imperative]] clauses do distinguish polarity unambiguously, with the suffix ''-go'' for commands and ''-nin'' for prohibitives:<ref name="Donohue2014"/>

{{block indent|1={{Interlinear|libuŋ ə-go|play do-IMP|'Play!'
}}{{Interlinear|libuŋ ə-nin|play do-PROH|'Don't play!'
}} }}

[[Copula (linguistics)|Nonverbal predicates]] can also be clearly negated, using the negator ''otoq'' ('is not') for identity or the negative existential ''qaʕ-u'' ('does not exist') for existence:<ref name="Donohue2014"/>

{{block indent|1={{Interlinear|tsi-je gimi|1SG-GEN money|'(That's) my money.'
}}{{Interlinear|tsi-je gimi otoq|1SG-GEN money NEG|'(That's) not my money.'
}} }}

For non-imperative verbal clauses, however, no dedicated negative morpheme exists. What Watters et al. (2006) had analyzed as negative suffixes, such as ''-aʕu'' and ''-daʕu'', are reanalyzed by Donohue et al. as combinations of the nominalizing suffix ''-da'' (expressing a "manifest characteristic") with the negative existential ''qaʕ-u''. The resulting construction literally asserts the nonexistence of a nominalized property. For example:<ref name="Donohue2014"/>

{{block indent|1={{Interlinear|gina g-əm-da qaʕ-u|3SG 3-eat-CHAR not.exist-IRR|'He/she did not eat.'
| abbreviations = CHAR: manifest characteristic
}} }}

Two pieces of evidence support the reanalysis. First, the clitic ''-ba'' ('also, even') can be inserted between the nominalized verb and the negative existential, indicating a word boundary rather than a single suffix. 

{{block indent|1={{Interlinear|gina g-əm-da-ba qaʕ-u|3SG 3-eat-CHAR-also not.exist-IRR|'He/she did not even eat'
| abbreviations = CHAR: manifest characteristic
}} }}

Second, the construction is incompatible with future time reference, which follows from the semantics of ''-da'', since it refers to an already-realized characteristic.<ref name="Donohue2014" />

{{block indent|1={{Interlinear|*goraq gina g-əm-da qaʕ-u|tomorrow 3SG 3-eat-CHAR not.exist-IRR|'He/she will not eat tomorrow'
| abbreviations = CHAR: manifest characteristic
}} }}

To express negation in future contexts, the only available strategy is the [[irrealis mood|irrealis]] suffix ''-u'', but this is inherently ambiguous between positive and negative readings:<ref name="Donohue2014"/>

{{block indent|1={{Interlinear|goraq gina g-əm-u-a|tomorrow 3SG 3-eat-IRR-OPT|'He/she will not eat tomorrow.' / 'He/she will eat tomorrow.'
}} }}

This situation is typologically unusual, since most known languages have some unambiguous way of negating verbal predicates across all tense contexts.<ref name="Donohue2014"/>

===Morphology===
Proto-Kusunda pre-root nominal prefixes can be categorized into a two-slot system, with the possessor prefix attached before the classificatory prefix, which in turn comes before the root noun (for example, *g-u-hu 'bone' and *g-i-dzi 'name').<ref name="Spendley"/>

{| class="wikitable"
!  !! possessor prefix (-2) !! classificatory prefix (-1)
|-
! 1st person
| *t- || *i- (external body parts, abstractions)
|-
! 2nd person
| *n- || *a-
|-
! 3rd person
| *g- || *u- (internal body parts), *ja- (human beings)
|}

The proposed class markers *i-, *a-, *u-, and *ja- are proposed to be triggered by the possessive-marking prefixes *t-, *n-, and *g-. The system is reminiscent of nominal morphology in the [[Great Andamanese languages]].<ref name="Spendley"/>
