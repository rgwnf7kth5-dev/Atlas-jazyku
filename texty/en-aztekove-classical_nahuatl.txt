'''Classical Nahuatl''', also known simply as ''Aztec'' or ''Nahuatl'', is a set of variants of [[Nahuatl]] spoken in the [[Valley of Mexico]] and central Mexico as a ''[[lingua franca]]'' at the time of the 16th-century [[Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire]]. Indigenous languages, including Classical Nahuatl, remained dominant until the time when [[New Spain]] (Mexico) became independent in 1821.<ref>{{Citation |last=de León |first=Lourdes |chapter=Indigenous Language Policy and Education in Mexico |date=2017 |title=Language Policy and Political Issues in Education |pages=415–433 |chapter-url=https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-319-02344-1_31 |access-date=2026-04-11 |publisher=Springer, Cham |language=en |doi=10.1007/978-3-319-02344-1_31 |isbn=978-3-319-02344-1|chapter-url-access=subscription }}</ref> However, during the subsequent centuries, it was largely displaced by [[Spanish language|Spanish]] and evolved into some of the modern [[Nahuan languages]] in use (other modern dialects descend more directly from other 16th-century variants). Although classified as an [[extinct language]],<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.ethnologue.com/show_language.asp?code=nci |title=Ethnologue summary for Classical Nahuatl |access-date=2006-06-09 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130218111931/http://www.ethnologue.com/show_language.asp?code=nci |archive-date=2013-02-18 |url-status=dead }}</ref> Classical Nahuatl has survived through a multitude of written sources transcribed by [[Nahua peoples]] and Spaniards in the Latin script.

Classical Nahuatl may be called ''Codical Nahuatl'' (when referring to the variants employed in the [[Mesoamerican codices]] through the medium of the [[Aztec script]]) or ''Colonial Nahuatl'' (if written in post-conquest documents in the [[Latin script|Latin alphabet]]).

==Classification==
Classical Nahuatl is one of the [[Nahuan languages]] within the [[Uto-Aztecan languages|Uto-Aztecan family]]. It is classified as a central dialect and is most closely related to the modern dialects of Nahuatl spoken in the valley of Mexico in colonial and modern times. It is probable that the Classical Nahuatl documented by 16th- and 17th-century written sources represents a particularly prestigious [[sociolect]]. That is to say, the variety of Nahuatl recorded in these documents is most likely to be more particularly representative of the speech of Aztec nobles ({{lang|nci|[[pipiltin|pīpiltin]]}}), while the commoners ({{lang|nci|[[Macehualtin|mācēhualtin]]}}) spoke a somewhat different variety.

===Accent===
[[Stress (linguistics)|Stress]] generally falls on the penultimate syllable. The one exception is the [[vocative]] suffix (used by men) {{lang|nci|-é}}, which is added to the end of a word and is always stressed, e.g. {{lang|nci|Cuāuhtli<u>quetz</u>qui}} (a name, meaning "[[eagle warrior|Eagle Warrior]]"), but {{lang|nci|Cuāuhtliquetz<u>qué</u>}} "O Cuauhtliquetzqui!"

When women use the vocative, the stress is shifted to the final syllable without adding any suffix. {{lang|nci|Oquichtli}} means "man", and {{lang|nci|oquichtlí}} means "O man!"

===Phonotactics===
Maximally complex Nahuatl syllables are of the form CVC;<ref>
{{Harvnb|Aguilar|2013}}, citing {{Harvnb|Andrews|2003}}, {{Harvnb|Bedell|2011}}, {{Harvnb|Brockway|1963}}, and {{Harvnb|Goller|Goller|Waterhouse|1974}}
</ref>
that is, there can be at most one consonant at the beginning and end of every syllable. In contrast, [[English language|English]], for example, allows up to three consonants syllable-initially and up to four  [[consonant]]s to occur at the end of syllables (e.g. ''<u>str</u>e<u>ngths</u>)'' (''ngths'' = {{IPA|/ŋkθs/}}).<ref>
{{cite book |last1=Aslam |first1=Mohammed |last2=Kak |first2=Aadil Amin |date=2011 |title=Introduction to English Phonetics and Phonology |url= |location= |publisher=Foundation Books |pages=60–68 |isbn=9788175968653 |chapter=4 - English Syllable Structure |doi=10.1017/UPO9788175968653.005}}
</ref>
Consonant clusters are only allowed word-medially, Nahuatl uses processes of both epenthesis (usually of {{IPA|/i/}}) and deletion to deal with this constraint.

For such purposes, ''tl'' {{IPA|/tɬ/}}, like all other affricates, is treated as a single sound, and not all consonants can occur in both syllable-initial and syllable-final position.

The sonorants /n/, {{IPA|/l/}} and {{IPA|/w/}} are [[devoiced]] in syllable-final position. Likewise, {{IPA|/j/}} is also devoiced and merged into {{IPA|/ʃ/}} in syllable-final position.{{sfn|Launey|2011|p=8}} The sonorant /m/ is the only one that is not devoiced in final position because it never appears in that position to begin with.{{sfn|Andrews|2003|p=27}}

==Writing system==
{{main|Nahuatl orthography}}
At the time of the Spanish conquest, Aztec writing used mostly [[pictogram]]s supplemented with a few [[ideogram]]s. When needed, it also used syllabic equivalences{{citation needed|date=January 2016}}; [[Diego Durán]] recorded how the {{lang|es|tlacuilos}} could render a prayer in [[Latin]] using this system but it was difficult to use. The writing system was adequate for keeping such records as genealogies, astronomical information, and tribute lists, but it could not represent a full vocabulary of spoken language in the way that the [[writing system]]s of the [[Old World]] or the [[Maya civilization]]'s [[Maya script|script]] could.

The Spanish introduced the [[Latin script]], which was then used to record a large body of Aztec prose and poetry, which somewhat diminished the devastating loss caused by the burning of thousands of [[Aztec codices]] by the Spanish authorities.

==Literature==
[[Mesoamerican literature|Nahuatl literature]] is extensive (probably the most extensive of all Indigenous languages of the Americas), including a relatively large corpus of [[poetry]] (see also [[Nezahualcoyotl (tlatoani)|Nezahualcoyotl]]). The {{lang|nci|[[Huei tlamahuiçoltica]]}} is an early sample of literary Nahuatl.

A [[bilingual]] dictionary with Spanish, {{lang|es|[[Vocabulario manual de las lenguas castellana y mexicana]]}}, was first published in 1611 and is "the most important and most frequently reprinted Spanish work on Nahuatl," according to the [[World Digital Library]].<ref>{{Cite web| title = Manual Vocabulary of the Spanish and Mexican Languages: In Which are Contained the Words, Questions, and Answers Commonly and Usually Found in the Treatment and Communication Between Spaniards and Indians | work = [[World Digital Library]] | access-date = 2013-05-23 | url = http://www.wdl.org/en/item/2836/}} {{dead link|date=May 2025}}</ref>
